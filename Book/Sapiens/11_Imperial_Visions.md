# 11 — Imperial Visions

## Key Idea

Almost everyone alive today is the descendant — culturally, linguistically, legally — of one empire or another. Harari opens with the Roman siege of Numantia: Spanish patriots still honour the Numantians as heroes of resistance — in Spanish, the conqueror's language, under Roman-derived law and institutions, inside a church whose leader sits in Rome. The victory was so complete that the victors co-opted the very memory of the vanquished.

The chapter defines empire coldly — cultural diversity plus flexible borders, not cruelty or size. It dismantles both halves of the modern indictment (that empires "don't work" and that they are simply evil), replacing outrage with a harder question: since imperial legacies are woven into every surviving culture, what would it even mean to purify ourselves of them? Empire has been history's most powerful fusion engine — and the order being forged in our own century is simply the latest imperial vision, one whose colour "may well be green."

## The Argument

- **The siege of Numantia and what counts as an empire.** In 134 BC Rome sent Scipio Aemilianus with 30,000 soldiers against the small Celtiberian town of Numantia. After more than a year the Numantians burned their town and mostly killed themselves rather than become slaves. Numantia then lived a second life as a symbol — Cervantes wrote a tragedy about it, its ruins became a national monument in 1882 — all homage paid in the victor's currency: a romance language descended from Scipio's Latin, Graeco-Roman literary models, a law and politics built on Roman foundations. An empire must rule a significant number of distinct peoples with different cultural identities and have flexible borders with a potentially unlimited appetite.

- **Do empires work — and are they evil?** The modern critique has two claims: empires cannot rule many conquered peoples effectively in the long run; and even if they could, they should not, being engines of destruction and exploitation. The first, Harari says, is "plain nonsense" — empire has been the world's most common political form for 2,500 years, and a stable one: rebellions were routinely crushed; empires usually fell only to external invasion or internal elite splits. Conquered peoples were usually slowly digested until their cultures fizzled out: when Rome fell in AD 476, no Numantian, Etruscan or Samnite nation crawled out of its carcass.

- **The benevolent imperial vision and when they become us.** Sargon's Akkadian Empire (c. 2250 BC) is the first we know of for certain, and his boast of conquering the world became a mantle claimed for 1,700 years by Assyrian, Babylonian and Hittite kings. Cyrus the Great of Persia (c. 550 BC) changed the formula: he claimed not merely to rule the whole world but to do so for the sake of all its people — "We are conquering you for your own benefit." Imperial ideology from Cyrus onward broke with evolution's instinctive xenophobia, affirming the unity of the world and mutual responsibility. Empires fused many small cultures into few big cultures — for convenience and for legitimacy. Yet assimilation stayed painful: the generations between conquest and acceptance lost their old culture while still being called barbarians — the Iberian merchant with flawless Latin who recites Virgil by heart, yet never lands a government post.

- **The imperial cycle and the new global empire.** Twentieth-century anti-colonial movements fought under banners — self-determination, socialism, human rights — that were themselves Western imperial legacies. It is tempting to file every empire under "bad guys," since most were founded on blood and kept by oppression — but most of today's cultures rest on imperial legacies. If empires are bad by definition, "what does that say about us?" The Raj cost millions of lives — yet the modern Indian state is its child. Most humans have lived in empires since about 200 BC, and most probably will again — except that the next one will be truly global. Governed by no single state or ethnic group, it resembles the Late Roman Empire — a multi-ethnic elite bound by common culture and interests.

## Key Concepts

### Empire

A political order with two defining traits: rule over a significant number of culturally distinct peoples, and flexible borders with a potentially unlimited appetite. Deliberately *not* defined by origins, government, extent or population — Athens, the Habsburgs, democratic Britain and the Aztecs all qualify. The definition turns "empire" from a swear word into an analytical category.

### The imperial vision and assimilation

The claim, innovated by Cyrus the Great, that an empire rules the whole world *for the benefit of all its inhabitants*. The slow conversion of conquered "they" into "us": empires standardise laws, writing, language and money, spread a hybrid culture in the name of benefit, and eventually absorb their subjects. The cruelty concentrates in the liminal generations, who have lost their old culture without being admitted to the new.

### Imperial legacies and the authentic-culture illusion

No culture alive today is pure: all are at least partly the legacy of empires, so the dream of cutting imperialism out of culture is fantasy — and in practice it sanctifies an older empire while denouncing a newer one (hating the Raj defends the Mughal inheritance; purging Islam sanctifies the Mauryas). The point is not that empires were kind, but that inheritance is a palimpsest no one can erase cleanly.

### The new global empire

Harari's name for the order taking shape around us: not a state but a multi-ethnic elite of experts, managers, lawyers and scholars, united by a common global culture and interests, while sovereign states lose real independence. Its founding myth will be environmental. It carries the book's unification thesis into the present.
