# 03 — A Day in the Life of Adam and Eve

## Key Idea

This chapter tries to answer the question the whole book keeps circling back to: what kind of creature are we, really? Harari's starting point is the claim of evolutionary psychology that our minds were shaped not in cities or on farms but on the savannah — for nearly the entire history of our species, Sapiens lived as foragers, and the two centuries of office work and the ten millennia of farming since are, by comparison, the blink of an eye. If we want to understand why we gorge on ice cream, why marriages fail, why we feel alienated inside our own abundance, we must get inside the heads of hunter-gatherers who died tens of thousands of years ago.

The chapter's deeper work is a lesson in intellectual honesty. The window it examines — from the Cognitive Revolution of 70,000 years ago to the dawn of agriculture about 12,000 years ago — left almost no record of its inner life, and Harari refuses to pretend otherwise: scholars cannot even agree whether our ancestors lived in jealous nuclear families or free-loving communes, since both camps argue from "flimsy evidence." The great reframe: the search for one "natural way of life" for humans is itself the mistake — after the Cognitive Revolution there was never a single natural way, only cultural choices.

## The Argument

- **Evolutionary psychology's claim.** Our present-day social and psychological traits were forged in the long pre-agricultural era and now misfire against post-industrial life. Its most famous exhibit: high-calorie sweets were nearly nonexistent for foragers (ripe fruit was the only sweet food available 30,000 years ago), so the instinct to gorge was hard-wired — and it explains today's obesity plague better than any moralizing about willpower.

- **The family debate.** Two rival reconstructions: the commune school (no private property, no paternity, communal parenting, modeled on chimpanzees and bonobos) versus the monogamy school (nuclear families as core human cells, explaining possessiveness and father-to-son succession). Harari's point is not to pick a side but to note that the evidence cannot settle it — no written records, biased archaeology, unreliable modern analogies. The honest conclusion is diversity: different bands almost certainly organized differently.

- **The original affluent society.** Scholars' label for pre-agricultural foragers, who combined a short working week (35–45 hours even in the Kalahari, hunting one day in three), light housework, a varied diet of dozens of wild foods, relative freedom from famine and from infectious diseases (which mostly arrived with domesticated animals), and bodies as fit as marathon runners — yet were no paradise: want, child mortality, lethal accidents, and cruelty were real. This is the book's key benchmark: a standard of living that agriculture, by Harari's later accounting, failed to beat.

- **The curtain of silence.** Harari's metaphor for everything the forager record cannot tell us: particular events, beliefs, political dramas — tens of thousands of years of possible history that left no trace. His insistence on asking unanswerable questions is itself a method: it protects the 60,000 years of pre-agricultural history from being dismissed as unimportant, and it frames the book's recurring humility about evidence.

## Takeaway

**We will never fully see Eden** — the curtain of silence is too thick — but the foragers behind it worked less, ate better, and knew more than we do, and every "natural way of life" we long for was just one choice among many.
