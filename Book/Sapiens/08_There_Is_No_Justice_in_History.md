# 08 — There Is No Justice in History

## Key Idea

This chapter is the moral reckoning of the imagined orders earlier chapters celebrated as the engine of civilisation. The question it starts from is the one the Agricultural Revolution left behind: how could millions of strangers cooperate in mass networks when humans have no biological instinct for it? The answer — imagined orders plus scripts — worked, but as a dubious blessing: those orders were never neutral. Each divided people into make-believe groups stacked in a hierarchy — privileges at the top, discrimination and oppression at the bottom. Hammurabi's Code and the American Declaration of Independence are Exhibit A: each proclaimed justice while consecrating slavery, subjection and the rule of the rich.

Nor is this a bug we can edit out: complex societies seem to *require* imagined hierarchies, because hierarchy lets strangers coordinate without ever knowing each other. What differs is only *which* fiction ranks people — caste in India, race in America, class nearly everywhere — and how accidental beginnings harden into rigid systems through self-reinforcing "vicious circles." It then turns to gender, the one hierarchy present in every known society — too universal to be a chance accident.

## The Argument

- **Mass networks need fictions — and fictions rank people.** After the Agricultural Revolution, imagined orders and scripts filled the gaps in our biological inheritance — but those orders were "neither neutral nor fair." Hammurabi's Code arranged superiors, commoners and slaves; the American order of 1776, despite proclaiming the equality of all men, ranked men over women, whites over blacks and Indians, and rich over poor. Every imagined hierarchy "disavows its fictional origins and claims to be natural and inevitable." Yet all of it is invention: no biological difference separates slaves from free people — or one race's intelligence from another's.

- **The vicious circle, Indian and American.** Why does one society rank by caste, another by race? Usually a chance event, perpetuated by vested interests. The caste system likely began when Indo-Aryan invaders subjugated northern India about 3,000 years ago and froze the population into occupational castes sealed by prohibitions on mixing, marriage and shared meals. African slavery began as pure circumstance: Africa was closer and cheaper, already had a slave market, and Africans carried partial genetic immunity to the malaria that killed European labourers. Nobody wanted to admit slavery was mere economics, so myths were pressed into service — and they outlived the institution. In Alabama after the Civil War, the Thirteenth Amendment was helpless against the machinery of stigma. When Clennon King, a black student, applied to the University of Mississippi in 1958 he was committed to a mental asylum.

- **The master hierarchy: gender.** Every known society divides humans into men and women; almost everywhere men have had the better deal since the Agricultural Revolution. A Chinese oracle bone from 1200 BC records Lady Hao's childbirth: "Not lucky. It was a girl." In many legal systems women were property: rape was a property violation against her male owner, settled by a bride price. As late as 2006, fifty-three countries still barred prosecuting husbands for raping wives. Around the hard kernel of biology — men lack wombs — every society has piled layers of culture with little biological basis. Harari's rule of thumb — "Biology enables, Culture forbids" — says it all.

- **Theories of patriarchy — and their collapse.** Because patriarchy predates contact between the hemispheres, it cannot be a vicious circle started by chance; there must be a universal cause, and we have not found it. Harari dismantles all three standard candidates. *Muscle power:* women are excluded mainly from jobs requiring *little* strength — priesthood, law, politics — while doing the hard fieldwork. *Aggression and war:* men fill the armies, but why must war's managers be men? Wars are complex projects of organisation, cooperation and appeasement. *Patriarchal genes:* evolution supposedly selected competitive men and submissive women — but among elephants and bonobos, dependent females and competitive males produce matriarchies. The honest conclusion — the most influential social hierarchy in history remains unexplained.

## Key Concepts

### Imagined hierarchies and the claim of naturalness

Imagined orders do not only coordinate people — they rank them, giving privileges to the top and burdens to the bottom. Harari's iron rule: every such hierarchy insists it is natural and inevitable, never admitting its invented origin. That claim is the load-bearing wall of social injustice — if a hierarchy is natural, questioning it is as futile as questioning gravity.

### The vicious circle

A chance historical situation translated into a rigid social system, each element reinforcing the others: stigma blocks advancement; the absence of the stigmatised from elite positions "proves" the stigma; the "proof" justifies fresh discrimination — until the circle hardens into law, myth and aesthetics. The examples are caste in India and race in America, and the moral is bleak: such circles usually *worsen* with time.

### "Biology enables, culture forbids"

Harari's rule of thumb for separating real biology from biological myth. Biology tolerates a wide spectrum of possibilities — women can bear children, men can enjoy sex with men — and culture decides which possibilities are realised and which are forbidden. Culture defends its prohibitions as "natural," but from biology's perspective nothing is unnatural: our very concept of the natural is inherited from Christian theology, with its purposeful Creator, while evolution has no purpose.

## Takeaway

No hierarchy is natural: each began as an accident and survives by convincing us it was inevitable — which is why history, not biology, is the only science that can explain human society, and the only lever that can change it.
