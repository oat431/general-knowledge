# 19: And They Lived Happily Ever After

## Key Idea

We have followed Sapiens through three revolutions and watched the species conquer the planet, and now, where a fairy tale would close, Harari asks whether "and they lived happily ever after" is even true. The last five centuries united the earth into a single ecological and historical sphere, grew the economy exponentially, and handed humankind superhuman powers and practically limitless energy. Yet the founding question is disarmingly simple: did any of it make us happier? Historians have researched just about everything, politics, gender, disease, food, clothing, and almost never how it all shaped human happiness. That silence is not innocent: nationalism, communism and capitalism each rest on unexamined claims about the source of contentment, so the happiness question is the one most worth asking and least asked.

The chapter then runs that question through three disciplines. Psychology and sociology measure happiness as "subjective well-being" and find that money, health and even freedom matter far less than family, community, and the gap between what we have and what we expect. Biology goes further: serotonin, dopamine and oxytocin set an emotional thermostat that history can barely move. Philosophy delivers the sting: meaning in life is, from a scientific viewpoint, delusion, so happiness may be little more than synchronising our personal fictions with the collective fictions around us. Against every modern answer, Harari holds up Buddhism's ancient one: stop chasing feelings and learn what you truly are.

## The Argument

- **The question historians avoid.** Harari opens with Neil Armstrong's footprint on the moon against a hunter-gatherer's handprint from 30,000 years ago. Who was happier? Historians never ask, yet every modern ideology is a bet on an answer. The common-sense view (more power means more happiness) fails on the book's own evidence: farming multiplied collective power while individual lives grew harsher. The romantic counter-view is equally dogmatic: modern medicine cut child mortality dramatically. The golden-age case has three cracks: the sample is tiny, we judge from one privileged viewpoint, and the wealth was built on the suffering of tens of billions of farm animals.

- **Psychologists count happiness.** Happiness is measured as "subjective well-being" on a zero-to-ten scale. The findings deflate our assumptions: money buys happiness only up to a point, family and community outweigh money and health, and happiness tracks not objective conditions but their fit with subjective expectations. Want a bullock-cart and get one, you are content; want a Ferrari and receive a second-hand Fiat, you feel robbed. This makes historical accounting nearly impossible: medieval peasants had exactly what they wanted, so on that score they were content. Modern society actively depletes contentment by stretching expectations against impossible standards.

- **Biochemistry sets the thermostat.** Biologists insist well-being is biochemical: moods are governed by serotonin, dopamine and oxytocin. Nobody is made happy by lottery wins or love, only by pleasant sensations in the body. Evolution never selected for happiness: our systems keep us neither too miserable nor too happy, like an air-conditioner fixed at twenty degrees. If this is right, history is nearly irrelevant: a medieval peasant and a 2014 banker both watch their serotonin climb to level X. The one lever that matters is manipulating biochemistry directly, Prozac instead of regimes.

- **Meaning and the Buddhist third path.** Kahneman's research unsettles the pleasure view: raising a child reads as drudgery episode by episode, yet parents insist their children are their chief source of happiness. So happiness may be the sense of one's life as meaningful. Yet from a scientific viewpoint, human life has no meaning, and any meaning we ascribe is a delusion. Happiness is synchronising your personal delusions with the prevailing collective ones. Buddhism, Harari suggests, has studied happiness longer and harder than any creed. It agrees with biology that happiness arises from processes inside the body, then diverges completely. Suffering's root is not pain but craving: feelings are fleeting vibrations, and our endless pursuit of pleasant ones keeps the mind tense. Liberation comes when the pursuit stops and feelings are observed without clinging. Buddha's deeper insight was that true happiness is independent even of inner feelings, because you are not your feelings.

## Key Concepts

### The expectation gap

Happiness depends not on objective conditions but on the correlation between conditions and subjective expectations. Want a bullock-cart and get one, you are content; want a Ferrari and receive a second-hand Fiat, you feel robbed. Expectations inflate with every improvement, so even dramatic progress leaves us dissatisfied. This wrecks historical accounting: contentment can never be read off wealth, health or politics, and media and advertising, which stretch expectations against impossible standards, become silent thieves of contentment.

### The biochemical set point

Biologists claim subjective well-being is governed by serotonin, dopamine and oxytocin, a system evolution tuned for survival and reproduction, not for happiness, which is why it holds each person near a personal set point, like an air-conditioner fixed at twenty degrees. Events may momentarily shift the mood, but the system returns to its setting. The consequence is radical: history can change the stimuli that release serotonin, but not the resulting levels, so no revolution, empire or economic miracle can make people happier, the only true lever is direct biochemical manipulation.

### Buddhism: happiness beyond feelings

Buddhism, Harari suggests, has studied happiness longer and harder than any creed. It agrees with biology that happiness arises from processes inside the body, not events outside, then diverges completely. Suffering's root is not pain but craving: feelings are fleeting vibrations, waves that rise and pass, and our endless pursuit of pleasant ones keeps the mind tense and dissatisfied. Liberation comes when the pursuit stops and feelings are observed without clinging. Buddha's deeper insight was that true happiness is independent even of inner feelings, because you are not your feelings.

## Takeaway

Five centuries of triumph may have changed everything about how humans live and almost nothing about how they feel, because happiness tracks expectations, biochemistry and shared meaning, not revolutions.
