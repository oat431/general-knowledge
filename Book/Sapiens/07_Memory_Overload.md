# 07 — Memory Overload

## Key Idea

Walk into any schoolyard and you can join a football game with ten strangers you have never met. Evolution gave us legs for kicking and mouths for cursing, but no genes for football — the rules live in our heads as an identical set of ideas, entirely imaginary, yet shared. Kingdoms, churches and trade networks run on exactly the same trick, with one fatal difference: the rules of football are simple enough to fit inside a single brain alongside songs and shopping lists, while the operating manuals of large societies are vastly bigger than any one mind. That bottleneck — **memory overload** — is this chapter's subject. Harari's central claim: before Sapiens could run cities, kingdoms and empires, we first had to invent a way to store and process information *outside* the brain.

This matters because the chapter is the hinge between the imagined orders of the previous chapters and the real kingdoms that fill the rest of the book: no empire could tax, count or redistribute agricultural surplus without an information layer to hold it. And it plants the book's deepest anxiety: every system we build to think outside our heads ends up reshaping the heads themselves.

## The Argument

- **The problem and why bees don't need lawyers.** Puppies have rough-and-tumble play hard-wired into their genes; human teenagers have no genes for football, yet play with strangers because everyone has learned the same imaginary rules. Ant and bee societies stay stable because their information is encoded in the genome. Humans are different: because our social order is imagined, it cannot be copied into DNA. Laws, customs and procedures must be sustained by conscious effort, or the order collapses.

- **Three defects of the brain and the Sumerian escape.** The brain fails as an empire database for three reasons: its *capacity* is limited; it *dies* (information erased in less than a century); it is *specialised* (evolution adapted it to store botanical, zoological and social information, not numbers). When complex societies appeared, a completely new type of information became vital: collecting taxes from hundreds of thousands of people demanded millions of data bits. The first to break through were the Sumerians of southern Mesopotamia. Between 3500 and 3000 BC they invented writing — a data-processing system combining two kinds of signs pressed into clay: numerals and signs for people, animals, merchandise and dates. It preserved more data than any brain could remember.

- **Signed, Kushim, and the retrieval problem.** Early Sumerian writing was confined to facts and figures. The oldest messages are humdrum economic documents like the tablet that reads: 29,086 measures of barley received over 37 months — signed, Kushim. If Kushim was a real person, he may be the first individual in history whose actual name we know. This was because the earliest writing was *partial script* — able to represent only particular types of information — not a *full script* that can express everything spoken. The new problem was retrieval: what do you do with thousands of clay tablets? Inscribing a document is not enough; you need catalogues, reproduction, retrieval methods and pedantic librarians. Inventing these proved far harder than inventing writing.

- **Scribe schools and the language of numbers.** Scribes learned catalogues, dictionaries, calendars, forms and tables — techniques of information handling alien to the brain. The brain stores everything in free association: signing a mortgage reminds you of your first home, then alligators, then Wagner. Bureaucracy demands one drawer for mortgages, another for marriage certificates, a third for tax registers. Its operators must be reprogrammed to think like filing cabinets. This is script's most important impact: it gradually changed the way humans think, as free association gave way to compartmentalisation. Writing's newest offspring, binary code, pushes the logic further: since computers struggle to understand how Homo sapiens talks and dreams, we are teaching Homo sapiens to talk and dream in numbers.

## Key Concepts

### Writing as external data-processing

Writing is a method of storing information through material signs — not, in origin, a recording of speech. The Sumerian system pressed two kinds of signs into clay, numerals and pictographs, deliberately built to handle mathematical data that spoken language could not hold. Its significance is architectural: information stored in clay no longer dies with its owner, and societies could finally cross the size threshold that the brain imposed.

### Full script versus partial script

Full script represents spoken language more or less completely and can express anything. Partial script handles only particular types of information — Sumerian numerals, mathematical notation, musical notation, quipus. The chapter's counterintuitive point: partial script, not full script, powered civilisation's machinery, because tax accounts and bureaucracies were born with it and remain welded to it.

### Bureaucratic thought

The mental style that external storage demands. The brain retrieves by free association; archives require strict separation of categories, each record in its drawer. People who run such systems must be "reprogrammed" to think like filing cabinets. The concept carries the chapter's boldest claim: the most important impact of script is cognitive. Technologies of storage rewrite the thinker.
