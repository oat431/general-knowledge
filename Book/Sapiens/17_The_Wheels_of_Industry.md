# 17 — The Wheels of Industry

## Key Idea

Chapter 16 gave the modern economy its fuel: credit, trust in the future, and profits reinvested in production. Chapter 17 opens with the obvious objection — growth also needs energy and raw materials, and those look finite: exhaust them, and the whole system collapses. The doomsday logic is familiar, yet it fits the historical record badly. Whenever a shortage has threatened to slow growth, investment has flowed into scientific research, which has delivered not just more efficient ways of using existing resources, but completely new kinds of energy and materials. Resources, Harari concludes, are finite only in theory.

The reframe quietly redefines the Industrial Revolution. We picture factories, chimneys, and exploited miners; Harari pictures **conversion** — heat into movement, movement into electricity, matter into energy. For almost all of human history the only machine that could convert one form of energy into another was the living body; the steam engine broke that monopoly, and the limit on human power became the limit of ideas in our heads. The chapter closes the economic story of this section — knowledge, capital, and energy fused into one self-feeding loop — and then shows what the loop ran over: farm animals mechanised into production lines, and humans re-educated as consumers who must keep buying.

## The Argument

- **The shortage that keeps failing to arrive.** Three hundred years of manufacturing billions of vehicles should have scraped the barrel dry. Instead, in 1700 the vehicle industry ran on wood and iron; today Toyota and Boeing factories run on petroleum and nuclear power, using materials — plastic, rubber, aluminium, titanium — our ancestors never knew. The old energies — wood, wind, water — all had limits, and nobody could convert one kind into another. Humans had exactly one conversion machine: the body. History was ruled by two cycles, of plant growth and of sunlight. When granaries stood empty, armies could barely move; when the wheat ripened, kings planned campaigns.

- **The secret in the kitchen and an ocean of energy.** For millennia people stood daily before the most important invention in the history of energy and failed to see it: the jumping lid of a boiling kettle was heat converting itself into movement. Around 1700 the steam engine appeared, pumping water from British coal mines. The principle is simple — burn fuel, boil water, let expanding steam push a piston. More important still, escaping the mines broke a psychological barrier: if coal could move looms, why not vehicles? In 1825 an engineer ran a steam engine pulling mine wagons along an iron rail — the first locomotive. The Industrial Revolution is a revolution in energy conversion that has repeatedly shown there is no limit to the energy at our disposal — only to our knowledge. The sun alone delivers 3,766,800 exajoules a year; all human activities consume about 500 — what the sun sends in ninety minutes. Cheap energy also dissolved the second bottleneck, scarce raw materials: aluminium was so costly that Napoleon III served it to his most distinguished guests and gold to everyone else, until late-nineteenth-century chemists made extraction cheap. Today 30 million tons a year end up as throwaway foil.

- **Life on the conveyor belt and the age of shopping.** The productivity explosion was felt first and foremost in agriculture: the Industrial Revolution was above all else the **Second Agricultural Revolution**. Tractors replaced muscle; artificial fertilisers, insecticides, hormones multiplied yields. But living creatures were mechanised too: just as humanist religions were elevating *Homo sapiens* to divine status, farm animals stopped being seen as sentient beings and became machines. Hens, whose nature urges them to scout and forage, are squeezed four to a cage; sows, among the most intelligent of mammals, are confined in crates so small they cannot turn around. The engine of this cruelty is not animosity but indifference. Before industrial agriculture, most food went to feeding the peasants who grew it. Today 2 per cent of Americans feed the whole country and export the surplus, and the billions of hands released from the fields staffed the factories and offices that pour out an unprecedented avalanche of products. For the first time in history, supply outstrips demand. A new problem is born: who is going to buy all this stuff? So a new ethic appeared: **consumerism**. Frugality had been the historic watchword; consumerism inverts all that: consuming more is a positive good, frugality is a disease to be cured. We buy countless products we did not know existed yesterday; manufacturers design short-term goods and unnecessary new models so we must buy to stay "in"; obesity now rivals starvation as the age's curse. Two commandments — invest; buy — are one coin. The creed is revolutionary in one final sense: older ethics demanded compassion and restraint that almost nobody achieved. This ethic asks only greed of the rich and craving of the masses — the first religion in history whose followers actually do what they are asked to do.

## Key Concepts

### Energy conversion and knowledge as the ultimate raw material

The ability to transform one kind of energy into another — heat into movement, movement into electricity, mass into energy. For most of history the only converter was the living body; every machine since replaces that metabolic trick. Conversion uncoupled human power from the daily solar ration. Harari's claim that only ignorance limits energy rests here — "inventing better pumps" becomes the motto. Raw materials are products of knowledge — so the limit that matters is ignorance.

### The Second Agricultural Revolution

The industrialisation of farming — tractors, fertilisers, insecticides, hormones, cold chains — and, Harari argues, the true core of the Industrial Revolution: it freed the peasant masses, over 90 per cent of every pre-modern society, to staff factories and offices. Its shadow is the mechanisation of animals — hens, sows, and cows treated as machines. A distinction drawn from evolutionary psychology: a need shaped in the wild — a calf's urge to bond and play — is still felt subjectively even when survival no longer requires it. Industrial agriculture meets objective needs while starving subjective ones — and the animals suffer.

### Consumerism

The ethic making the consumption of ever more products a positive duty, invented to absorb the industrial avalanche of goods. It inverts historic frugality and completes, rather than contradicts, the capitalist creed: the rich are commanded to invest, the masses to buy. Harari calls it a quasi-religion — the first whose followers actually obey — which is why it is so hard to resist, and so hard to criticise from inside.

## Takeaway

The wheels of industry never stalled for lack of fuel or materials, because knowledge kept inventing both — the costs of the ride were paid instead by animals, by the poor, and by a consumer ethic nobody can resist.
