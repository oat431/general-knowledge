# 15 — The Marriage of Science and Empire

## Key Idea

Chapter 14 left us with a civilisation that began by admitting what it did not know. This chapter shows the confession only became world-conquering when it married empire: the Scientific Revolution and modern imperialism were inseparable — one enterprise seen from two ends of the same ship, scientists needing money and data, empires needing knowledge and legitimacy.

The emblem is Cook's 1768 voyage to Tahiti: pure science — a timed transit of Venus — commanded by a naval officer who claimed Australia along the way. That fusion answers the chapter's riddle — why a frigid backwater of Eurasia conquered the world — and darkens the tale: the same alliance produced the extermination of the Tasmanians, scientific racism, and the world we still live in. The empires have died; science and capital, their offspring, have not.

## The Argument

- **Why Europe and why so late?** The puzzle: around 1500, western Europe was a provincial backwater compared to China, India, or the Islamic world. Yet within three centuries Europeans dominated the globe. The answer is not technology — Europeans had no decisive edge in weapons, ships, or navigation over the Ottomans or Chinese. The difference was a mindset: the willingness to admit ignorance and the ambition to conquer the unknown. Pre-modern empires (Roman, Arab, Mongol, Aztec) conquered to spread their culture or plunder wealth; European empires conquered to discover. This made them uniquely voracious learners and uniquely destructive.

- **The empty map and the parochialism penalty.** Pre-modern world maps had no blank spaces — unknown regions were filled with monsters or omitted, claiming total knowledge. The European map full of empty spaces was a public admission of ignorance that turned cartography into a research programme. Columbus could not admit the Bible had missed half the world; Vespucci said we don't know and got a continent named for it. The parochialism penalty is the price of assuming you already know the world: the Aztecs knew nothing of the Caribbean genocide on their doorstep, the Incas nothing of Mexico's fate, the Asian empires heard of America and did nothing — while Europeans, expert at invading lands about which they were ignorant, kept gathering the knowledge that made each conquest cheaper.

- **Imperial science and the legacy of racism.** Systematic knowledge-gathering as an instrument of rule and self-justification: the Great Survey of India, Mohenjo-daro's excavation, cuneiform decipherment, comparative linguistics. It governed (a few thousand Britons ruled 300 million Indians partly by knowing them better than they knew themselves), legitimised (new knowledge branded empire progressive) and could horrify (Tasmanian corpses in museums, the Aryan race theory). Scientific racism — the belief that biological differences justify domination — was a product of this imperial science. When biology debunked race, empire adapted: culturism is Harari's coinage for the respectable successor of imperial racism: differences between peoples now attributed to culture and history rather than biology. Harder to refute than racism: biologists can show biological differences are trivial, but scholars of culture cannot call cultural differences trivial without abolishing their own disciplines — which is why culturism survives in respectable politics.

## Key Concepts

### The marriage of science and empire

Harari's name for the symbiosis that made both partners world-conquering: science and empire grew up hand in hand, each supplying what the other lacked. The Cook question — science under military protection, or the military with scientists along? — has no answer, because those involved could not tell the difference; every later revolution inherits the fused institution.

### The empty map and the parochialism penalty

Pre-modern world maps had no blank spaces — unknown regions were filled with monsters or omitted, claiming total knowledge. The European map full of empty spaces was a public admission of ignorance that turned cartography into a research programme. ### Imperial science and from racism to culturism
