# 14: The Discovery of Ignorance

## Key Idea

This chapter opens with a strange pair of time-travellers. A medieval peasant who fell asleep in AD 1000 and woke in 1500 would have found the world quite familiar; but one of Columbus's sailors, waking to a twenty-first-century iPhone, would have wondered whether he had landed in heaven or hell. Harari pours out the numbers, population up fourteen-fold since 1500, production 240-fold, daily energy consumption 115-fold, but the chapter's real subject is not the growth. It is the idea that made it possible.

Every earlier tradition assumed the important answers were already known; modern science is the first that openly confesses ignorance, our best theories may be wrong, so the Scientific Revolution was not a revolution of knowledge but of ignorance, launched by the discovery that humans do not know the answers to their most important questions. The chapter defines modern science (admit ignorance; observe and measure; acquire new powers), shows how it hooked itself to politics and economics in a self-reinforcing loop, and traces the creed it produced, belief in progress, culminating in an open campaign against death.

## The Argument

- **Five centuries of compound growth.** The power surge shows in every register: 500 million humans in 1500 became 7 billion; annual production grew from $250 billion to about $60 trillion. In July 1969, an organism left footprints on the moon. At 05:29:45 on 16 July 1945, the first atomic bomb detonated, humankind gained the capability not only to change history but to end it. Before roughly 1500 nobody expected research to pay. Modern rulers believed the opposite, invest in research and you gain new powers, and the belief was repeatedly proven.

- **The discovery of ignorance.** Modern science differs from all earlier traditions in three ways: it admits ignorance, it relies on observation and mathematics, and it pursues new powers. Premodern traditions held that everything important was already known; they admitted only individual ignorance and ignorance of unimportant things. Modern science breaks the pattern by admitting collective ignorance about the most important questions: Darwin never claimed to be the last word on life, biologists still confess they cannot explain consciousness, and physicists admit they do not know what caused the Big Bang. This candour made science dynamic, but it created a social problem, if the shared myths that bind millions are open to doubt, how does society cohere? Modern orders answer with two unscientific methods: freezing some science into absolute truth (the Nazi and Communist way) or grounding order in a non-scientific absolute (liberal humanism).

- **Observation, mathematics, and knowledge as power.** In 1687 Newton published *The Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy*, showing that three simple laws predict the motion of everything from falling apples to planets. But biology, economics and psychology resisted neat equations, so science built a new mathematics for complexity: statistics. The case study is 1744 Scotland: two Presbyterian clergymen wanted a pension fund for ministers' widows. Instead of prayer they hired a mathematician and mined death records, Bernoulli's Law of Large Numbers says single deaths are unpredictable but averages are not. Their prediction that the fund would hold £58,348 by 1765 missed by exactly one pound. In 1620 Francis Bacon published his manifesto *The New Instrument*, arguing that "knowledge is power." Since no theory is ever fully certain, truth is a poor test of knowledge, the real test is utility. Bacon's deeper move was marrying science to technology, fields entirely separate before 1500 and firmly knotted only in the nineteenth century.

- **Progress, the Gilgamesh Project, and the sugar daddy of science.** The confession of ignorance changed the arithmetic: if our worst problems are simply fruits of ignorance, new knowledge can dissolve them. Poverty followed: Jesus told his disciples the poor would always be with them, but moderns treat want as a technical problem. The hardest problem is death itself. Premodern faiths made death the source of meaning and promised an afterlife rather than trying to escape it. Scientists reject that wisdom: death is not a decree but a series of technical failures, and every technical problem has a technical solution. The record backs the audacity: life expectancy climbed from roughly twenty-five to forty years to sixty-seven worldwide. Science is expensive and its wonders were paid for by governments, businesses and donors. Most research is funded because someone believes it serves a political, economic or religious goal. The sugar daddy of science is always some ideology or interest, which is why the next two chapters turn to empire and capitalism.

## Key Concepts

### Ignoramus, the founding confession

Modern science rests on the Latin injunction *ignoramus*, we do not know. It assumes not merely that knowledge is incomplete but that anything we believe could be proven wrong: no concept, idea or theory is sacred. The innovation was declaring collective ignorance about the most important questions legitimate.

### The Scientific Revolution's feedback loop

Harari's figure caption gives the chapter its engine: science, politics and economics reinforce one another. Political and economic institutions supply the resources; research returns new powers; those powers obtain new resources; some are reinvested in research. This loop explains why premodern rulers got stagnation and modern ones got compound growth.

### Knowledge is power, truth tested by utility

Bacon's slogan compresses the modern epistemology: because no theory is ever fully correct, truth is a poor test of knowledge, the real test is whether a theory empowers us to do new things. This explains the late, fateful wedding of science and technology, separate realms before 1500, firmly married only in the nineteenth century.

### The ideal of progress and the Gilgamesh Project

The belief that tomorrow can be better than today, through knowledge and invention rather than divine intervention, is itself a modern invention. The Gilgamesh Project, named from humanity's oldest myth, is the quest for immortality. The ancient Gilgamesh failed and returned with the wisdom that mortality is destiny; modern science replies that death is a bundle of technical failures awaiting technical fixes.
