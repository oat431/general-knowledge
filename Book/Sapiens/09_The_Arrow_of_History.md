# 09: The Arrow of History

## Key Idea

This is the chapter where Harari stops examining single societies and turns his gaze on the whole planet, asking two deceptively simple questions: what is culture, and does history have a direction? His answer to the first overturns a comfortable image, culture is not a fixed essence that defines a people forever, but a restless web of shared fictions that is *always* changing, pushed from within by its own contradictions. His answer to the second is an emphatic yes: seen from far enough away, history moves relentlessly from many small, isolated human worlds toward one big, interconnected world. The chapter opens the book's third movement, the unification of humankind, by showing that unification is not a recent accident of globalization but a millennial current that was already visible thousands of years ago.

It matters for the whole vault because it supplies the book's large-scale plot. Everything after the Agricultural Revolution, bigger societies, grander imagined orders, writing, empires, money, world religions, becomes legible once you see it as humanity being pulled, willingly or not, toward a single global culture. Harari closes by announcing the three "universal orders" that first imagined all humans as one, economic, political, and religious, and by pointing forward to the next chapters, which trace how money, empires, and universal religions actually spread.

## The Argument

- **From static cultures to cultures in flux.** Early twentieth-century scholars treated each culture as complete and harmonious, changed only by outside force. Today's scholars hold the opposite: every culture is in constant flux, and the engine of change is inside. Unlike the laws of physics, which contain no inconsistencies, every man-made order is packed with internal contradictions, and cultures spend their whole existence trying to reconcile them. The test case is the European nobility, who believed simultaneously in Christianity and in chivalry. A nobleman heard the priest preach that riches and honour are temptations, then dressed in silks for a castle banquet where the minstrel sang of Lancelot and Guinevere. The contradiction was never resolved, but the *effort* to resolve it changed the culture: it produced the Crusades, the Templars, and much of medieval literature.

- **Liberty vs. equality and dissonance as a feature.** The same machinery runs modern politics. Since the French Revolution the world has prized both equality and individual freedom, yet the two contradict each other, because guaranteeing equality requires curtailing the freedom of the better off. Harari reads the whole political history since 1789 as a series of attempts to reconcile this pair. Crucially, these contradictions are no defect, they are culture's engines. Harari then names the human capacity this requires: cognitive dissonance, usually treated as a failure of the psyche, is actually a vital asset, without the ability to hold contradictory beliefs, no culture could ever have been established.

- **The spy satellite and counting the human worlds.** If cultures are always churning, is the churn random or does it point somewhere? At the micro level, the "bird's-eye view" of decades, history looks like a random walk: the Mongol Empire swelled across Asia and shattered. But zoom out to the "cosmic spy satellite" that scans millennia and the pattern snaps into focus: small, simple cultures steadily coalesce into bigger, more complex civilizations. Around 10,000 BC there were many thousands of separate human worlds. By 2000 BC, only hundreds. By AD 1450, close to 90 per cent of all humans already lived inside a single Afro-Asian mega-world linked by cultural, political, and economic ties. The remaining tenth was spread across four sizable worlds: the Mesoamerican, the Andean, the Australian, and the Oceanic. The rest were dwarf worlds like Tasmania, cut off from the Australian mainland around 10,000 BC when rising seas stranded a few thousand hunter-gatherers, who then lived out twelve millennia in total ignorance of the rest of humanity.

- **The giant swallows the worlds and the end of authenticity.** Over the next three centuries the Afro-Asian giant consumed every other world: the Mesoamerican in 1521, the Oceanic beginning the same year, the Andean in 1532, and the Australian when British colonization began in earnest in 1788. Today virtually all humans share the same geopolitical, economic, legal, and scientific systems. If "authentic" means developed independently, free of external influence, then no authentic culture remains. The proof is in the pantry: the "ethnic" cuisines of the world are all recent hybrids. Tomatoes, chilli peppers, and cocoa are Mexican in origin and reached Europe and Asia only after the conquest of Mexico.

- **From "us and them" to universal orders.** Why did unity win? Evolution built Homo sapiens to divide the world into us and them; no social animal serves its whole species. The Cognitive Revolution let strangers cooperate as imagined brothers and friends, yet every brotherhood still stopped at a border. The breakthrough came in the first millennium BC with three *potentially universal* orders whose devotees could finally imagine the entire world governed by a single set of laws: the monetary order (economic), the imperial order (political), and the universal religions, Buddhism, Christianity, Islam. Merchants saw one market, conquerors one empire, prophets one truth.

## Key Concepts

### Internal contradiction as culture's engine

Every man-made order is packed with internal contradictions, and cultures change because they are constantly struggling to reconcile them. The chapter's paired examples are chivalry vs. Christianity in medieval Europe and liberty vs. equality since 1789. Contradiction is not a flaw to be fixed but the source of cultural creativity: discord in values compels thought, reevaluation, and criticism.

### Cognitive dissonance as a vital asset

Holding contradictory beliefs and incompatible values at the same time is such an essential feature of belonging to any culture that psychology gave it a name: cognitive dissonance. Usually judged a failure of the psyche, Harari argues it is a vital asset, had people been unable to hold contradictions, establishing and maintaining any culture would likely have been impossible. The lesson extends outward: to really understand another culture, investigate its catch-22s, the spots where its rules are at war.

### The three universal orders

In the first millennium BC, three potentially universal orders appeared whose devotees could, for the first time, imagine the entire human race as a single unit under a single set of laws: the **monetary order** (economic), the **imperial order** (political), and the **universal religions**, Buddhism, Christianity, and Islam. Their carriers were merchants, conquerors, and prophets, the first people to transcend the evolutionary "us vs. them" division.
