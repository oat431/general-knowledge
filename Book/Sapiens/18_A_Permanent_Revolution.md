# 18 — A Permanent Revolution

## Key Idea

Most of us picture the Industrial Revolution as a story of machines. Harari looks past the hardware: within two centuries, industrial civilisation rewired the two most basic arrangements of human existence — how we keep time and whom we depend on — and changed the social order itself from something rigid and eternal into something in permanent flux. That is the permanent revolution of the title: not one upheaval like 1789 or 1917, but an order whose only constant is change.

His most startling move comes later: after opening with mass extinction, he argues that the industrial era was also the most peaceful in history, by a wide margin — the state ended local feuds, nuclear weapons made superpower war suicidal, wealth became too intangible to loot. The familiar bloodbath-to-bloodbath reading of recent history, he insists, mistakes the puddles for the dry land.

This completes the book's portrait of the scientific revolution: the engine of knowledge, credit and industry did not stop at laboratories and banks — it rebuilt daily life, the family and the very idea of order. And it poses the question the final chapters inherit: was the trade worth it?

## The Argument

- **Conquest of the biosphere and modern time.** Humanity moulded the world to its needs, measured in biomass: 7 billion humans at 300 million tons, farm animals at 700 million, all surviving large wild animals under 100 million. The revolution's first experiment in social engineering was time itself: medieval life ran on natural cycles, but industry sanctified precision. Public transport forced standardisation — railways adopted Greenwich time in 1847, Parliament legislated national time in 1880, making Britain the first country obliged to live by an artificial clock.

- **The collapse of the family and the community.** The era's most momentous social revolution was the collapse of the family and local community, replaced by the state and the market. For over a million years humans lived in intimate kin groups that provided welfare, health, education, security — the family was everything. The state and market offered a deal: become an individual, and we will provide what your family once did. Harari inverts the romantic cliché: the state and market are the individual's parents now, supplying schools, banks, mortgages, pensions, police. Women and children became individuals too. The cost is alienation; the gain is that transferring functions from kin and feud to courts and police drained violence from daily life.

- **Imagined communities and the elastic order.** People still need tribal bonds, so states and markets manufacture them: imagined communities of millions of strangers. The two modern kinds are the nation (imagined community of the state) and the consumer tribe (imagined community of the market). Nations hide their imagined character behind primordial soil and blood, yet most are industrial-era creations. Traditionally the social order was rigid; in two centuries it became dynamic, malleable, permanently in flux. Modern politics promises to destroy the old world and build a better one — and often delivers. Since 1945 the world combined unprecedented change with the widest margin of peace in history: the elastic order absorbed radical change without collapsing into war.

- **Peace, counted.** We underestimate this peace: in 2000, war and violent crime killed 830,000 people — fewer than the 1.26 million lost to car accidents or 815,000 suicides. Real peace is not the absence of war but its implausibility: the old iron law — any two neighbours always had a plausible war scenario within a year — no longer holds. Four factors explain it: nuclear weapons made superpower war suicidal; wealth became human capital and know-how, too intangible to loot; trade made peace pay; and a peace-loving elite sees war as evil and avoidable. Since 1945 no UN-recognised state has been conquered and wiped off the map.

## Key Concepts

### Industrial time and the collapse of the family

The uniform, precise schedule of the machine, imposed first on factory workers and then on everyone. Harari's candidate for the most momentous social revolution in history: within two centuries, the state and the market took over the welfare, health, education, security, insurance and matchmaking roles that families and communities had performed for over a million years. The loss is real — alienation, loneliness. But the gain is real too: transferring those functions from kin and feud to courts and police drained the violence out of daily life.

### Imagined communities and perpetuum mobile

Communities of millions of strangers who imagine they know one another. The two great modern kinds are the nation (imagined community of the state) and the consumer tribe (imagined community of the market). Neither is a lie; both are intersubjective realities, as real as money or human rights. The new character of the social order: permanent flux. Where premodern order meant stability, modern society exists in constant, engineered change — politics now sells the destruction of the old world and the building of a better one, and the elastic order absorbs radical reforms that older orders could only have met with civil war.

### Real peace

Not the absence of war but its implausibility — a condition no earlier era achieved. Four mutually reinforcing causes: nuclear weapons raised war's price, intangible wealth cut its profits, trade made peace pay, and a peace-loving elite made war illegitimate. The feedback loop's end product is the global empire foreseen in Chapter 11 — interdependent states too entangled to fight. It reframes modernity's balance sheet: an age of unprecedented slaughter was also, by the numbers, the most peaceful in history.

## Takeaway

**The Industrial Revolution's deepest product was not goods but a new kind of human order** — clocked time, atomised individuals, imagined communities and an elastic society that made the age of permanent upheaval the most peaceful in history.
