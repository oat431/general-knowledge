# 17 — Hindu Mythology — เทพปกรณัมฮินดู (รามายณะและมหาภารตะ)

> *"What is found here may be found elsewhere; what is not found here will not be found elsewhere." (traditional saying about the Mahabharata)**

This note (17) is the mythology twin of [[05_Hinduism]]: the religion side covered belief and practice; here we cover the two great epics (นิทานมหาระบำ) that carry Hindu civilization's imagination — the **Ramayana** (รามายณะ) and the **Mahabharata** (มหาภารตะ). And this is the vault's gold bridge: **the Ramayana is the source of รามเกียรติ์**, the epic King Rama I compiled and re-imagined for Thailand, which Thai students read in the ภาษาไทย classroom ([[ภาษาไทย/วรรณคดีและวรรณกรรม/สมัยรัตนโกสินทร์/01_รามเกียรติ์|รามเกียรติ์]]). When a child discovers that ทศกัณฐ์ is Ravana, that หนุมาน is Hanuman leaping across the ocean in the Indian original too, and that the shadow-plays of Nang Yai and the masks of Khon are one story traveled two thousand kilometers — world literature, religion, and Thai heritage meet in a single plot.

An adult should care because these are among the longest and most-influenced poems ever composed: the Mahabharata is roughly ten times the combined length of the Iliad and Odyssey; the Ramayana's Rama-Sita story refounds itself every Diwali across a billion people and every Khon season in Bangkok; and the Bhagavad Gita — a 700-verse episode inside the Mahabharata — is the most translated religious text in India's history, read by Gandhi, Oppenheimer, Thoreau, and Huxley each for different reasons.

## 1 | The Ramayana (รามายณะ): the journey of the ideal man

| Layer | Detail |
|---|---|
| Traditional author | The sage Valmiki (วาลมีกิ), "first poet" (adi kavi); legend says the bird-of-prey episode that sparked the meter |
| Date of composition | c. 500 BCE-100 BCE core, growing to c. 400 CE; multiple recensions (North, South, Bengali; Jain and Buddhist versions too) |
| Size | ~24,000 verses, seven books (kandas) |
| The genre | Itihasa ("thus it was"): Hinduism's epic-memory class, with the Mahabharata ([[World Literature/02_Ancient_Epics|Ancient Epics]]) |
| Central question | What does dharma cost a good man? (The Mahabharata asks what dharma costs a good *world*) |

### The story, book by book

| Book | Thai name | Events |
|---|---|---|
| Bala Kanda | พาล | Rama (ราม), prince of Ayodhya, an incarnation (อวตาร) of Vishnu born as a mortal to die Ravana; the childhood exploits; winning Sita (สีดา) at her swayamvara (การเลือกคู่) by stringing Shiva's bow |
| Ayodhya Kanda | อยุธยา | King Dasharatha (ท้าวทศรถ) exiles Rama for 14 years to keep a promise to his second wife Kaikeyi — dharma as the poison of a vow kept too well; Sita and Lakshmana (พระลักษณ์) follow; the kingdom's grief; Bharata (พรต) rules as regent with Rama's sandals on the throne |
| Aranya Kanda | อรัญญ | The forest years; the golden deer (the magic deer that is the demon Maricha in disguise); Ravana (ทศกัณฐ์), ten-headed king of Lanka, abducts Sita while Rama and Lakshmana are drawn away; Jatayu the vulture dies fighting for her |
| Kishkindha Kanda | กิษกินธ์ | The monkey alliance: Hanuman (หนุมาน) meets Rama; Sugriva's crown recovered; the monkey army (วานร) searches for Sita |
| Sundara Kanda | สุนทร | Hanuman's great leap across the ocean; finds Sita in Ravana's grove in Lanka; burns the city and returns with her necklace and message — the most beloved book; Hanuman as the perfect servant (ภักติ) |
| Yuddha Kanda | ยุทธ | The bridge of stones (built by the army; Nala sets floating stones); the siege and war of Lanka; Kumbhakarna and Indrajit (เมฆนาถ/อินทรชิต) fall; Rama kills Ravana with the Brahma-arrow; Sita's ordeal by fire (ไฟ) to prove her purity, and the triumphant return as Diwali's homecoming |
| Uttara Kanda | อุตตร (appendix) | The darker coda: gossip about Sita's honor banishes her, pregnant, to Valmiki's hermitage, where she bears twin sons Lava and Kush (ลพ-กุศ) who sing the epic at the horse-sacrifice and are reunited with their father; Sita asks the earth — her mother — to take her back, and it opens |

### Rama as maryada purushottam (พระรามผู้เป็นยอดแห่งธรรม)

Rama is not "perfect": he is a man for whom keeping dharmic obligation overrides every private good — the exile, the killing of Vali from hiding, the abandonment of Sita — and the poem refuses to look away from what that costs. The devotional tradition reads the flaws as the deliberate mystery of a god playing human; the literary reading sees the tragedy of an ideal that devours the people around it (Sita, Lakshmana, Bharata all pay). Both readings coexist, which is why the epic still argues with you a thousand years after it was written.

### The รามเกียรติ์ bridge (the vault's gold cross-link)

| Valmiki / Indian version | รามเกียรติ์ / Thai version | What the Thai telling changes |
|---|---|---|
| Rama (Rama) | พระราม (Phra Ram) | The ideal man becomes the ideal *king*: a model for Ayutthaya and Bangkok kingship (the Ramakien kingship cult) |
| Sita, daughter of the earth | นางสีดา | Her birth from the furrow, raised as a princess: the earth-mother thread kept |
| Ravana the rakshasa | ทศกัณฐ์ (Thotsakan) | Ten-headed, twenty-armed giant king of Lanka; his devotion to Shiva (the dance, the Lingam) and his comical family life (the giant wives and mother Mandodari) make him more character than monster — the best-dressed role in Khon |
| Hanuman the vanara | หนุมาน | Celibate but endlessly amorous in Thai scenes; Hanuman as national mascot of loyalty and mischief (the monkey general with a wife in every act) |
| Kumbhakarna | กุมภกรรณ | The sleeping giant, brother of Thotsakan |
| Indrajit/Meghanada | เมฆนาถ (อินทรชิต) | The thunder-god's son, the weeping giant warrior — one of the Ramakien's most celebrated episodes (เมฆนาท) |
| Vibhishana | พิเภก | Thotsakan's righteous brother who defects to Rama's side; in the Ramakien he is crowned king of Lanka and becomes the prototype of loyal dissent |
| The bridge and war | สะพาน and the Lanka siege | The same campaign; but Thai landscape names, Thai weapons (พระขรรค์ and the Chakri vs the Indian astras), and Theravada-Himmapan cosmology (เขาพระสุเมรุ, forest Himmapan, the chakrawat world-map) re-map the war onto Thai geography |
| Ravana's Lanka | ลงกา (Lanka) | The giant city, whose model for temple murals was the Thai court's own imagination (plus real Ceylon memory) |
| The ending | รัตนโกสินทร์ addition | Rama I's version extends to Rama's later life and the lineage of Thai kings: the Ramakien is the dynasty's mirror and charter |

> **Why this matters for the Thai learner:** the ภาษาไทย classroom note ([[ภาษาไทย/วรรณคดีและวรรณกรรม/สมัยรัตนโกสินทร์/01_รามเกียรติ์|รามเกียรติ์]]) and this note are two halves of the same text. The Ramakien is not a "translation" of the Ramayana — it is a re-composition (reshaped characters, new episodes, Thai morals), exactly as the story was re-composed in Cambodia (Reamker — note the name!), Laos (Phra Lak Phra Lam), Burma (Yama Zatdaw), Indonesia, Malaysia (Hikayat Seri Rama), and the Philippines. Southeast Asia owns this epic as much as India does, and it's the best single lesson in how myth migrates (note 12 §1, note 19).

## 2 | The Mahabharata (มหาภารตะ): the war that ended an age

| Layer | Detail |
|---|---|
| Traditional author | Vyasa (วยาส), "the compiler," a character inside his own epic |
| Size | ~90,000+ verses (Sloka); "whatever is here is found elsewhere" — an encyclopedia, not a novel |
| Frame | The sage Vaisampayana recites it at Janamejaya's snake-sacrifice; the telling has layers of listeners (and the narrator as a character in the frame) |
| Central question | Dharma in a corrupt system: what does righteousness do when every option is wrong — the age of Kali Yuga (กลียุค, the fourth and worst of the yugas) |

### The story in one table

| Phase | Events |
|---|---|
| The family | Two branches of the Bharata dynasty: the Pandavas (the five sons of Pandu: Yudhishthira, Bhima, Arjuna, the twins Nakula and Sahadeva) and the Kauravas (the hundred sons of Dhritarashtra, led by Duryodhana). Shared tutors, shared cousin-hood, and an inheritance dispute that becomes the frame |
| The dice game | Yudhishthira, the righteous gambler, loses his kingdom, brothers, and wife Draupadi in a rigged dice match; Draupadi's public humiliation (her disrobing answered by Krishna's cloth that never runs out) makes the crime unfixable; the Pandavas accept 13 years of exile |
| The preparation | Exile adventures, divine weapons acquired, Duryodhana refuses to return even "five villages as land enough"; every peace attempt fails; Krishna's embassy fails |
| Kurukshetra (กุรุเกษตร) | An 18-day war; Bhishma falls on a bed of arrows, Drona falls by a half-truth, Karna falls by a curse and a stuck wheel; the club-war of Bhima and Duryodhana closes the field; almost everyone on both sides is dead |
| The after | Gandhari's curse on Krishna; the Pandavas' brief, unhappy reign; the fratricidal end of the Yadava clan (Krishna's own family) and his death — the age closes |
| Ascent | The Pandavas walk toward heaven one by one, falling for their one flaw each; Yudhishthira alone passes the dog (righteousness wearing animal form) to a heaven that contains the enemy — the final moral: there is no side that had all the good |

### The Bhagavad Gita (ภควัทคีตา) inside it

On the first morning of the war Arjuna refuses to fight (his kinsmen fill the enemy lines), and Krishna — his charioteer, who is Vishnu's eighth avatar — answers (the full treatment in [[05_Hinduism]] §3):

| Teaching | Content |
|---|---|
| Karma yoga | Act from duty without attachment to fruit: "you have a right to action, never to its fruits" |
| The soul | The self is unborn and undying; "who thinks it kills, and who thinks it dies — neither knows the truth" — the metaphysics that makes a warrior's grief survivable |
| The three yogas | Action, knowledge, devotion — doors for every temperament |
| The Vishvarupa | Arjuna begs to see Krishna's cosmic form and is granted it — all time and all beings in one mouth; the vision is beautiful and annihilating: "now I am become Death, the destroyer of worlds," as Oppenheimer quoted it in 1945 |

### The comparative frame: why the epics pair

| | Ramayana | Mahabharata |
|---|---|---|
| Genre | Romance-adventure in one family's moral line | A dynastic war in an entire world |
| Hero | Rama: the man who keeps dharma | No single hero; Arjuna/Krishna/Yudhishthira share the burden; the anti-hero Karna is the tragic favorite |
| Answer | Dharma can be kept, at terrible personal cost | Dharma cannot be kept clean in a complex world; act anyway |
| Greek twin | The Odyssey | The Iliad |

## 3 | How the epics live: performance and the modern world

| Form | Where | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Ramlila | North India, every autumn | Staged Ramayana cycles before Diwali; whole cities attend for days |
| Kathakali | Kerala | The Ramayana's climactic battle in painted mask-dance; Rama and Ravana as codified roles |
| Khon and Lakhon | Thailand | รามเกียรติ์ masked dance; the National Theatre on Sanam Chai performs it nightly; the Ramakien murals of the Grand Palace's gallery walk the whole plot |
| Wayang kulit | Java/Bali | Shadow-play (หนังใหญ่) versions of *both* epics; the Pandavas as Javanese princes; UNESCO-recognized |
| Burmese Yama Zatdaw, Khmer Reamker, Lao Phra Lak Phra Lam | SE Asia | National epics on the same bones — each with a Thai-style twist (note 1 §"why it matters") |
| Television, comics, novels | India 1987 Ramayan serial; R. K. Narayan's retellings; Amish Tripathi's Shiva trilogy; the 2020s TV re-runs broke viewing records in lockdown | The epics as pop: Arjun the student, Sita as feminist, the Gita as self-help (see [[20_Mythology_in_Modern_Culture]]) |
| Worship | The Ramayana as scripture in temples; daily recitation for merit; Diwali's Ravana effigies burned | Story as ritual (the §"charter function" of [[12_What_Is_Mythology]]) |

## 4 | Key concepts and vocabulary

| Term | Thai | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Itihasa | อิติหาส | "So it was": the epic-history genre; source of Thai/SE Asian royal genealogies and cosmology |
| Dharma | ธรรม | The plot's true protagonist in both epics |
| Ravana's boons | ทศกัณฐ์ | The ten-headed demon-king's near-invincibility from austerities to Brahma and Shiva — the epic's engine |
| Swayamvara | พิธีเลือกคู่ของเจ้าหญิง | The princess's self-choice ceremony at which Sita is won (and, in the Ramakien, the coronation-adjacent ceremony the classroom calls พระราชพิธีเลือกคู่) |
| Vanara | วานร | The monkey-people army; not "monkeys" but forest-folk and tree-spirits in the epic's own anthropology |
| Rakshasa | อสูร/ยักษ์ | The man-eating anti-gods; Ravana's race, with a civilization, priests, and Vedas of their own (the epic's irony: the good kings sometimes hunt them without reason) |
| Astras | ศรเทพ | Divine weapons, mantra-powered: the Brahma-arrow, the Pashupata; not guns but prayer-tech |
| Kurukshetra | ยุทธภูมิทุ่งกุรุ | The field of the war, still a pilgrimage site in Haryana |
| Yuga | ยุค | The four world-ages: the Mahabharata closes the Dvapara and opens the Kali Yuga (กลียุค) in which we live |
| Avatar | อวตาร | The deity's deliberate descent: Rama and Krishna (Vishnu's 7th and 8th of the ten) — see [[05_Hinduism]] §5 |
| Draupadi | Draupadi | Wife of all five Pandavas (a polyandrous epic fact that still shocks and theorizes); the dice-game's plaintiff; the woman whose humiliation ends a world |
| Karna | กรรณะ | The secret elder brother of the Pandavas, tragic hero of the loser's side, the most loved modern character |

## 5 | Why it matters today

- **The Thai connection above all:** รามเกียรติ์ is a required text through the Thai curriculum; Khon is a living UNESCO art; the Chakri dynasty's names, the Ayutthaya temple program, the Bangkok murals — the Ramayana is Thai heritage in a borrowed dress, and this note + the ภาษาไทย classroom note is the full bridge.
- **The world's largest living festival-literature:** more people attend Ramlila, watch Khon, or perform Diwali's Ravana-burning annually than attend any theater event in the Western canon; the epics are performance, not books.
- **The moral laboratory:** the Mahabharata's "no clean side" is a better preparation for reading history and news than most modern ethics courses — and the Gita's "act without attachment to results" is a serious alternative Stoic-style therapy (see [[Philosophy/06_Hellenistic_Philosophy]]).
- **Migration of myth is the lesson of the age:** the same story becomes Indian, Thai, Khmer, Lao, Burmese, and Javanese — and each tells it better for its own people — the clearest proof that culture transfers are creative, not corrupt (the comparative method of note 12; see [[20_Mythology_in_Modern_Culture]]).

## 6 | Further reading

- *The Ramayana* in R. K. Narayan's short retelling (quick) or Robert Goldman's Princeton translation (the full Valmiki, scholarly).
- *The Mahabharata* in R. K. Narayan's abridgment (one weekend) or the Kisari Mohan Ganguli / Bibek Debroy full translations.
- *Bhagavad Gita As It Is* for the devotional reading, or Winthrop Sargeant's literal edition for the academic one — and Gandhi's *Gospel of Autobiography* chapters on it for the political reading.
- The Ramakien: King Rama I's version in Thai (Royal Institute / National Library editions); in English, the complete translation published by the Social Science Association of Thailand (Bangkok). For viewing: the National Theatre's Khon performances and the Grand Palace gallery murals walk the full plot.
