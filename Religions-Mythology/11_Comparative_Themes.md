# 11 — Comparative Themes — หัวข้อเปรียบเทียบ

> *"The dogmas of religion are the answers given by the human spirit to the questions posed by the human spirit." (adapted from Rudolf Otto's framing, via Huston Smith)**

This note (11) is the domain's synthesis: it puts the traditions of notes 01–10 side by side across the questions every religion answers — how the world began, what happens after death, how to treat your neighbor, why leave home, why deny the body, who mediates the sacred. Two ground rules keep comparison honest: describe each tradition's answer **in its own terms first** (the error of "parallelomania" is seeing the same thing where different things merely look alike), and hold both similarity *and* difference at once — the golden rule appears everywhere *and* means something slightly different everywhere.

An adult should care because comparative literacy is the operating system of interfaith life: it lets a Thai Buddhist recognize a Muslim neighbor's Ramadan as a cousin of their own uposatha fasting without collapsing the two, and it inoculates against the two popular errors — "all religions are the same" and "my religion is the only real one."

## 1 | Creation: how the world began

| Tradition | The story | Type |
|---|---|---|
| Mesopotamian | Marduk slays the salt-water dragon Tiamat and splits her body into sky and earth ([[16_Mesopotamian_Mythology]]) | Combat-creation (chaos monster) |
| Egyptian | Atum/Ra self-generates on the first mound out of the waters Nun; speech/thought makes things ([[15_Egyptian_Mythology]]) | Emergence from primal water |
| Hindu | The Nasadiya Sukta (Rig Veda 10.129) wonders whether even the gods know: "who really knows whence it arose?"; later Puranas tell Brahma, the cosmic egg, and the sacrifice of the primordial being (Purusha) | Skeptical hymn + sacrifice-from-body |
| Chinese | Pangu (盘古) splits the cosmic egg, holds heaven and earth apart until he dies, his body becoming the world; or the Dao begets the One, the Two, the Three ([[07_Chinese_Religious_Traditions]], [[18_East_Asian_Mythology]]) | Body-of-the-giant / emanation |
| Greek | Chaos → Gaia → the Titans → Zeus divides the cosmos by lot after overthrowing them ([[13_Greek_Mythology]]) | Succession myth (generations of gods) |
| Norse | Odin and brothers slay the giant Ymir, make the world from his corpse; Midgard from his brows ([[14_Norse_Mythology]]) | Body-of-the-giant (combat) |
| Abrahamic | Genesis: one God speaks creation from water-and-darkness in six days; John's gospel recasts Christ as the cosmic Word through whom all things were made; the Quran repeats: the heavens and earth were a joined thing, We split them ([[04_Judaism]], [[02_Christianity]], [[03_Islam]]) | Creation by word (creatio) |
| Zoroastrian | Ahura Mazda creates in two stages — spiritual then material, the six Amesha Spentas presiding over the elements ([[09_Zoroastrianism]]) | Seven-part staged creation |
| Indigenous | Diverse: Earth-diver (the animal who dives for mud); emergence through successive worlds; the Dreaming songlines that sang the land into being ([[10_Indigenous_and_Animist_Traditions]]) | Emergence / dive / song |

**Patterns worth naming:** from water, from a body, by a word, by a fight, by a song. The comparative question isn't "which is right" but "why do so many traditions start with dark water?" (flood-adjacent memory, agricultural observation, and the psychology of depth all compete as answers — see [[19_Universal_Mythic_Archetypes]]).

## 2 | The afterlife: what happens when we die

| Tradition | Mechanism | Destination(s) | Body involved? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Egyptian | Weighing of the heart against Ma'at's feather; Ammit devours the failed ([[15_Egyptian_Mythology]]) | The Field of Reeds (a better Egypt); or non-existence | Yes — mummification keeps the ka/ba tied to the body |
| Zoroastrian | Chinvat Bridge judged by thoughts/words/deeds; final renewal of all ([[09_Zoroastrianism]]) | House of Song / House of Lies, corrective not eternal | Yes — bodily resurrection at the end |
| Abrahamic | Divine judgment by faith and works (the mixes differ by tradition) | Heaven / hell (and purgatory in Catholic teaching; Jannah's gardens and Jahannam in Islam) | Resurrection of the body (creeds in all three, varying emphasis) |
| Hindu / Buddhist | Karma, no external judge needed | Rebirth in samsara; moksha/nibbana ends the round | The cycle is of bodies; liberation is *escape* from the body-pattern |
| Chinese folk | The soul's bureaucratic audit; meritorious or sinful record; Qingming sustains the ancestors | Ancestor-hood, heavens, or the court of the underworld; purgatory-like refinements | Dual souls (hun/po) with different fates |
| Greek | Unjudged except for the famous sinners | Hades' gray shade-land for nearly all; Elysium for the exceptional | Shades only; the body is ash — a notable exception to moralized afterlives |
| Norse | Fame and the hall, not morality | Valhalla (battle-dead with Odin), Fólkvangr (Freya), Hel (the ordinary dead) | Corporeal halls await; even the gods die at Ragnarök |
| Indigenous | The dead stay related | Ancestor-spirit around the family; or a sky/feature path (Plains: the pipe road; Māori: the departure at Cape Reinga) | Relationship persists more than place |

**Comparative insight:** the moralized afterlife (judgment by *conduct*) is historically late and uneven: classical Greece barely has it, Homer's Hades is democratic gray. Egypt, Zoroaster, and then the Abrahamic line made death a courtroom — and once that frame exists, every tradition within reach borrows it or reacts against it.

## 3 | The golden rule: how to treat your neighbor

| Tradition | Statement | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Confucian | "Do not impose on others what you do not wish for yourself" | Analects 15.24 ([[07_Chinese_Religious_Traditions]]) |
| Hindu (Mahabharata) | "What is disagreeable to oneself, do not do unto others" | Anushasana Parva |
| Buddhist | "A state of hostility... a heart full of hate: the way of the noble is not this. A heart boundless toward all the world" — and "do not hurt others in ways you yourself would find painful" | Udana-vagga, Samyutta Nikaya |
| Jewish | Hillel: "What is hateful to you, do not do to your neighbor. That is the whole Torah" | Talmud, Shabbat 31a ([[04_Judaism]]) |
| Christian | "Do to others whatever you would like them to do to you" — the positive form | Matthew 7:12; and love your enemy |
| Muslim | "None of you truly believes until he wishes for his brother what he wishes for himself" | Hadith, Nawavi 13 |
| Zoroastrian | "That is only good nature which does not do to another what is not good for oneself" | Denkard 3.68 |
| Sikh | "Vand shako" — share; and "recognize the whole human race as one" | Guru Granth Sahib ([[06_Sikhism]]) |
| Native (Lakota) | "All my relations" (Mitákuye Oyás'iŋ) — the rule extended to the more-than-human world | Prayer formula ([[10_Indigenous_and_Animist_Traditions]]) |

**The two honest readings:** perennialists (Huxley, Smith) see one truth in many costumes; particularists (Prothero) note each tradition's *center* differs — the golden rule is a shared *floor*, not the house. Both are true, and the difference between "they agree on ethics" and "they agree on everything" is the most useful sentence in comparative religion.

## 4 | Prayer and meditation: technologies of the sacred

| Form | Where | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Petition/intercession | Abrahamic, folk Hindu, indigenous | Asking the personal Other for help; communal and private |
| Liturgy (set prayers, canonical hours) | Jewish Amidah at fixed hours, Christian Liturgy of the Hours, Muslim five salat, Hindu sandhya | Time sanctified by repetition; prayer as a schedule (compare: the Thai monastic day's fixed chants) |
| Contemplation/meditation | Buddhist samatha/vipassana, Hindu dhyana/yoga, Christian centering prayer and hesychasm, Sufi dhikr, Quaker waiting | Quieting the self to see (or be) what is real |
| Mantra/name-recitation | Hindu japa, Sikh naam simran, Pure Land Buddhist nenbutsu, Jesus Prayer | The Name does the work; sound as vehicle |
| Ecstatic/possession | Sufi whirling, Vodou ceremony, shamanic trance, spirit-mediumship in Thai and Balinese ritual | The self is ridden by something other — the most controversial form across traditions |
| Meditation-as-civic | Vipassana centers, mindfulness clinics, Zen in performance coaching | The forms stripped of their theology; a live comparative question (does Buddhist meditation work the same without the Three Jewels? see [[Social Studies/01 Religion/02_Buddhist_Ceremonies_and_Meditation|Buddhist Ceremonies]]) |

## 5 | Fasting: the disciplined body

| Tradition | Fast | Purpose stated |
|---|---|---|
| Islam | Ramadan, dawn to sunset, one month | Taqwa (God-consciousness), empathy, gratitude (see [[03_Islam]]) |
| Christianity | Lent (40 days, varying), Orthodox fasts ~half the year, earlier Friday abstinence | Repentance, solidarity with Christ's passion, simplicity |
| Judaism | Yom Kippur (25h), Tisha B'Av, minor fasts | Atonement; mourning; affliction and return |
| Hinduism | Ekadashi, Navaratri, Shravan Mondays, festival-specific | Purity, devotion, self-mastery; many fasts are *for* a deity's pleasure |
| Buddhism | Uposatha days; no-eating after noon (monastic) | Renunciation, wakefulness, monastic discipline — not hunger as offering |
| Sikhism | Fasting discouraged by the Gurus (Gobind Singh's code) | Truthful living beats ritual denial; service instead |
| Indigenous | Vision-quest fasting; Ramadan-analogous communal fasts for rain or mourning | Power-quest; humility before the land |
| Zoroastrian | Historical; some days of abstinence tied to the calendar | Self-control as cosmic alignment |

**Pattern:** fasting converts hunger from a fact into a language. The comparative edge: Sikhism is the rare tradition that *rejects* ritual fasting outright — proof that even a shared practice is contested, not universal.

## 6 | Pilgrimage: leaving home to find the center

| Journey | Tradition | The site | What the walking does |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hajj | Islam | Mecca and the surrounding plain | Re-enacts Hajar and Ibrahim; equality in white; the ummah made visible (see [[03_Islam]]) |
| Kumbh Mela / the char dham | Hinduism | The rivers at Allahabad, Haridwar, Ujjain; four cardinal shrines | Darsana; bathing burns karma; the largest gatherings on Earth (note 05) |
| Camino de Santiago / Marian sites / the Holy Land | Christianity | Santiago, Lourdes, Fatima, Jerusalem | Penance and petition; the body walking as prayer |
| Israel's three festivals (Pesach, Shavuot, Sukkot) | Judaism | Jerusalem's Temple (historically); now synagogue and home | The commanded pilgrimage that became the home-centered festival |
| Shikoku 88 / Ise | Japanese (Buddhist-Shinto) | The 1,200 km island circuit; Ise's great inner shrine | Walking with the begging vow; the shrine rebuilt every 20 years |
| the Sun Dance / the hills / the river | Indigenous nations | Sacred geography itself | The land's story entered by the feet |
| (and the critics) | all | inward turn | Reform Judaism redirected pilgrimage to ethical living; Quakers made the journey inward; the pattern proves the form is contested even where it is strongest |

**Comparative insight:** pilgrimages cluster the sacred into a *place*, then make the cost of reaching it part of the benefit. The modern secular version (birth-country tours, war-memorial treks, concert pilgrimages) suggests the form survives its gods.

## 7 | Ethics, law, and salvation: three styles

| Style | Name | Where | The organizing question |
|---|---|---|---|
| Orthopraxy-heavy ("right practice") | Nomological/ritual | Rabbinic Judaism (halakha), Hindu dharma (varnashrama), Confucian li, Islam's fiqh, Shinto purity | "What must I *do*, and how?" |
| Orthodoxy-heavy ("right belief") | Creedal | Christianity (the creeds decide membership), Nicene-heritage Islam's articles of faith | "What must I *believe*?" |
| Transformation-heavy ("right being") | Soteriological/mystical | Buddhism (liberation by understanding), Sufism, Vedanta, Christian theosis | "What must I *become*?" |

Most traditions mix all three; which bottle dominates explains a lot of intra-faith argument (Protestant vs Catholic = creed vs practice; Theravada vs Pure Land = effort vs faith). See [[01_What_Is_Religion]] §6 for the orthodoxy/orthopraxy axis.

## 8 | Sacred time and calendar

| Move | Example |
|---|---|
| Weekly rest | The Jewish-Shinto-Islamic-Thai-secular Sabbath arc: from the Babylonian "evil day" to the universal weekend |
| Annual re-actualization | Passover: "in every generation one should see himself as if he came out of Egypt"; Eid; Easter; Songkran; Loy Krathong |
| Lunar vs solar tension | Hijri (pure lunar) vs Hebrew (lunar-solar) vs Chinese (lunisolar) vs Buddhist (lunar observance days, uposatha) — calendars are comparative theology in arithmetic |
| Festivals of light | Diwali, Hanukkah, Loy Krathong, Christmas: the light-against-darkness cluster, timed near the year's deepest nights |
| Death-commemorations | Qingming, Obon, Farvardinan, All Souls' Day, Chinese Zhongyuan: nearly universal, the ancestor-cycle pattern |

## 9 | Why it matters today

- **Interfaith at kitchen-table level:** Thai neighborhoods mix Buddhist, Muslim, Christian, Chinese-folk, and spirit-house households. The tables above give a family the exact vocabulary to say: "Ramadan and our uposatha both teach the body a lesson — and we fast differently."
- **Reading fundamentalisms:** every "us-versus-them" movement in a tradition is arguing with its own comparative floor — Hindu-nationalist vs Gandhi's Vedanta, Christian nationalist vs the Sermon on the Mount, Salafi vs Sufi. Comparative literacy means knowing which internal voice is speaking.
- **Ethics without religion:** the golden-rule table is the standard argument both for a shared global ethic (the 1993 Parliament of the World's Religions declaration used exactly this method) and against it (a floor is not a foundation). Either way it's the table every UN and ASEAN interfaith event rebuilds by hand.
- **For the mythology half:** everything above has mythic doublets — creation and flood stories, dying-and-rising gods, tricksters — handled in [[12_What_Is_Mythology]] and [[19_Universal_Mythic_Archetypes]].

## 10 | Further reading

- *The World's Religions* (Huston Smith): the respectful comparative classic; its final chapter is this note's ancestor.
- *God Is Not One* (Stephen Prothero): the necessary corrective; argues the traditions' differences matter more than the agreement.
- *The Masks of God, vol. I: Primitive Mythology* (Joseph Campbell): the comparative pattern-book — read with the §3 caution about over-reading sameness.
- *Christian Faith and Other Faiths* (Jane I. Smith) and her *What is Sacred? What is Religious?*: how a committed insider compares honestly; Smith's method transfers to Buddhist and Muslim scholars doing the same.

*\* the phrasing condenses Otto's distinction as Smith restates it.*
