# Behavioral Biology — พฤติกรรมวิทยา

> *"The behavior of an organism is the sum total of its responses to internal and external stimuli."* — Niko Tinbergen

Behavioral biology (พฤติกรรมวิทยา) examines how and why organisms behave as they do. Behavior (พฤติกรรม) is the observable response of an organism to stimuli in its environment. It ranges from simple reflexes to complex social interactions. The study of animal behavior (ethology, จริยวิทยา) was pioneered by Nikolaas Tinbergen, Konrad Lorenz, and Karl von Frisch, who shared the 1973 Nobel Prize for their work.

Behaviors are classified as **innate** (พฤติกรรมแต่กำเนิด) — genetically programmed and present without learning — or **learned** (พฤติกรรมที่เรียนรู้) — modified by experience. Both types have evolutionary significance: innate behaviors provide immediate survival advantages, while learned behaviors allow organisms to adapt to changing environments. The interplay between nature (genes) and nurture (environment) shapes the full behavioral repertoire of an organism.

## 3 | Key Concepts

### 3.1 Innate Behavior (พฤติกรรมแต่กำเนิด)

Innate behaviors are genetically determined, stereotyped, and performed correctly the first time without prior experience.

- **Reflexes** (รีเฟล็กซ์): simplest responses to stimuli (e.g., knee-jerk reflex, withdrawal from pain). Involves a reflex arc: receptor → sensory neuron → integration center → motor neuron → effector
- **Taxes** (แทกซิส): directional movement toward or away from a stimulus. **Positive phototaxis** (movement toward light) in moths; **negative geotaxis** (movement against gravity) in fish larvae
- **Instincts** (สัญชาตญาด): complex, fixed action patterns (FAPs) triggered by a specific stimulus (sign stimulus/releaser). Example: egg-retrieval behavior in greylag geese (Lorenz), web-spinning in spiders

### 3.2 Learned Behavior (พฤติกรรมที่เรียนรู้)

Learned behaviors result from experience and can be modified:

- **Habituation** (การปรับตัว): decreased response to repeated, harmless stimuli. Example: birds ignoring scarecrows after repeated exposure
- **Classical conditioning** (การวางเงื่อนไขแบบคลาสสิก): Pavlov's dogs — associating a neutral stimulus (bell) with an unconditioned stimulus (food) to produce a conditioned response (salivation)
- **Operant conditioning** (การวางเงื่อนไขแบบกระทำ): Skinner's box — learning through consequences (reinforcement and punishment)
- **Imprinting** (การพิมพ์ตรา): rapid, irreversible learning during a critical period. Lorenz demonstrated that goslings imprint on the first moving object they see after hatching
- **Insight learning** (การหยั่งรู้): problem-solving without trial-and-error. Example: Kohler's chimpanzees stacking boxes to reach bananas

### 3.3 Migration (การอพยพ)

Seasonal, long-distance movements between breeding and non-breeding habitats. Examples: Arctic terns (pole to pole), salmon (ocean to natal stream), monarch butterflies. Navigation cues include:
- **Sun compass**: using the sun's position
- **Magnetic field**: detecting Earth's magnetism (magnetoreception)
- **Star patterns**: nocturnal migrants use star navigation
- **Landmarks**: familiar geographic features

### 3.4 Communication (การสื่อสาร)

Animals communicate through:
- **Chemical** (pheromones): ant trail pheromones, moth sex pheromones
- **Visual**: courtship displays, threat postures, color patterns
- **Auditory**: bird songs, whale calls, frog mating calls
- **Tactile**: grooming, honeybee waggle dance (combines tactile and chemical)

### 3.5 Social Behavior (พฤติกรรมทางสัง셜)

Social behaviors involve interactions between individuals of the same species:
- **Altruism** (พฤติกรรมเห็นแก่ประโยชน์ผู้อื่น): self-sacrificing behavior explained by kin selection (Hamilton's rule: rB > C)
- **Dominance hierarchies**: pecking orders in chickens, wolf packs
- **Territoriality**: defending a fixed area against rivals
- **Eusociality**: extreme cooperation with division of labor (ants, bees, termites)

### 3.6 Biological Rhythms (จังหวะชีวภาพ)

Internal biological clocks regulate cyclic behaviors:
- **Circadian rhythms** (~24 hours): sleep-wake cycle, body temperature, hormone release. Controlled by the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) in the hypothalamus
- **Circannual rhythms** (~1 year): migration, hibernation, breeding seasons
- **Ultradian rhythms** (<24 hours): heartbeat, breathing
- **Infradian rhythms** (>24 hours): menstrual cycle (~28 days)

Zeitgebers (time-givers) such as light and temperature entrain biological clocks to environmental cycles.

## 4 | Common Problem Types

### Type 1: Classifying Behaviors
> Classify the following behaviors as innate or learned: (a) spider spinning a web, (b) dog salivating at the sound of a bell, (c) human newborn rooting for breast, (d) crow using a tool to extract insects.

**Solution:**
1. (a) Spider spinning a web → **Innate** (instinct/fixed action pattern — genetically programmed)
2. (b) Dog salivating at bell → **Learned** (classical conditioning — Pavlov's experiment)
3. (c) Newborn rooting reflex → **Innate** (reflex — present at birth without experience)
4. (d) Crow using tools → **Learned** (insight learning or observational learning)

### Type 2: Tinbergen's Four Questions
> Apply Tinbergen's four questions to explain why a stickleback male attacks a red-bellied model.

**Solution:**
1. **Causation (proximate mechanism)**: Hormonal changes during breeding season trigger aggression toward red coloration
2. **Development (ontogeny)**: The behavior develops as the male matures and testosterone levels rise
3. **Function (adaptive value)**: Attacking rivals protects the male's territory and nest, increasing reproductive success
4. **Evolution (phylogeny)**: Sticklebacks evolved this response because males that defended nests against rivals produced more offspring
