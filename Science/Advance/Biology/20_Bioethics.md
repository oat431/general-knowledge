# Bioethics — ชีวจริยธรรม

> *"Science can tell us how to do something, but not whether we should."* — Leon Kass

Bioethics (ชีวจริยธรรม) is the study of ethical issues arising from advances in biology and medicine. As biotechnology accelerates — from gene editing to artificial intelligence in healthcare — society faces increasingly complex moral questions. Bioethics draws on philosophy, law, and social science to evaluate the moral implications of biological research and its applications.

The field is guided by four foundational principles: **autonomy** (สิทธิ์ในการตัดสินใจ), **beneficence** (การทำความดี), **non-maleficence** (ไม่ทำอันตราย), and **justice** (ความยุติธรรม). These principles frame debates over cloning, GMOs, stem cell research, genetic privacy, gene editing, animal rights, and informed consent. In Thailand, Buddhist ethics (จริยธรรมทางพุทธศาสนา) also significantly influence bioethical discourse.

## 3 | Key Concepts

### 3.1 Ethical Frameworks in Bioethics

The four foundational principles (Beauchamp and Childress):

- **Autonomy** (สิทธิ์ในการตัดสินใจ): Respect for individuals' right to make informed decisions about their own bodies and health. Requires **informed consent** (ความยินยอมอย่างรู้เท่าทัน) — patients must receive complete, accurate information before agreeing to treatment or research participation.

- **Beneficence** (การทำความดี): The obligation to act in the best interest of the patient or research subject. Research should aim to produce beneficial outcomes for society.

- **Non-maleficence** (การไม่ทำอันตราย): "Primum non nocere" — first, do no harm. Weigh risks against benefits before any intervention.

- **Justice** (ความยุติธรรม): Fair distribution of benefits and burdens. Ensuring that vulnerable populations are not exploited and that access to treatments is equitable.

### 3.2 Cloning Ethics (จริยธรรมการโคลนนิง)

**Reproductive cloning** (การโคลนนิงแบบสืบพันธุ์) — creating a genetically identical organism — raises concerns about:
- Human dignity and individuality
- Unknown long-term health risks (Dolly the sheep developed premature arthritis)
- Potential for exploitation

**Therapeutic cloning** (การโคลนนิงแบบบำบัด) — creating embryos for stem cells — is debated because:
- It involves creating and destroying human embryos
- Potential for treating diseases (Parkinson's, spinal cord injuries)
- Alternative: induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) avoid embryo destruction

### 3.3 GMO Debate (ข้อถกเถียงเรื่อง GMO)

**Arguments for GMOs:**
- Increased crop yields and food security
- Reduced pesticide use (Bt crops)
- Enhanced nutrition (Golden Rice with vitamin A)
- Potential for drought/heat-resistant crops amid climate change

**Arguments against GMOs:**
- Unknown long-term health and environmental effects
- Gene flow to wild relatives creating "superweeds"
- Corporate control of food supply (seed patents)
- Biodiversity loss from monoculture
- Consumer right to know (labeling debate)

### 3.4 Stem Cell Research (จริยธรรมการวิจัยเซลล์ต้นกำเนิด)

- **Embryonic stem cells** (ESC): most versatile but require embryo destruction
- **Adult stem cells**: less controversial but limited differentiation
- **iPSCs**: reprogrammed adult cells — ethically preferable but still experimental
- Key question: When does moral status begin? At conception, implantation, gastrulation, or birth?

### 3.5 Gene Editing Ethics (จริยธรรมการตัดต่อยีน)

CRISPR-Cas9 raises unique ethical concerns:
- **Somatic editing** (editing body cells): generally accepted for treating genetic diseases
- **Germline editing** (editing eggs, sperm, or embryos): heritable changes raise concerns about "designer babies," unintended consequences, and consent of future generations
- The 2018 He Jiankui case (editing human embryos in China) was widely condemned as premature and unethical
- Distinction between therapy (curing disease) and enhancement (improving traits)

### 3.6 Genetic Privacy (ความเป็นส่วนตัวทางพันธุกรรม)

- Genetic information can reveal predispositions to diseases, ancestry, and family relationships
- Concerns about discrimination by employers and insurers (GINA Act in the US)
- DNA databases used in forensics raise privacy and consent issues
- Direct-to-consumer genetic testing (23andMe) — who owns genetic data?

### 3.7 Animal Rights (สิทธิสัตว์)

- **Utilitarian view** (Peter Singer): minimize suffering for the greatest number; animal experimentation acceptable only if benefits outweigh suffering
- **Rights-based view** (Tom Regan): animals have inherent value and rights; experimentation violates these rights
- **3Rs principle** (Russell and Burch): Replacement, Reduction, Refinement
- In Thailand: Animal Welfare Act พ.ศ. 2557 (2014) prohibits cruelty

### 3.8 Informed Consent (ความยินยอมอย่างรู้เท่าทัน)

Essential elements:
1. **Disclosure**: all relevant information provided
2. **Understanding**: patient/comprehends the information
3. **Voluntariness**: no coercion or undue influence
4. **Competence**: patient is mentally capable of deciding
5. **Consent**: explicit agreement (written when possible)

Special considerations for vulnerable populations: children, prisoners, mentally incapacitated individuals, developing countries in clinical trials.

## 4 | Common Problem Types

### Type 1: Applying Ethical Principles
> A pharmaceutical company wants to test a new drug in a developing country with lower regulatory standards. Evaluate this using bioethical principles.

**Solution:**
1. **Autonomy**: Are participants fully informed? Can they truly give voluntary consent given power imbalances?
2. **Beneficence**: Will the drug benefit the local population, or only the company?
3. **Non-maleficence**: Are safety standards equivalent to those in developed countries?
4. **Justice**: Exploiting lower standards violates justice — equal protection should apply regardless of location
5. **Conclusion**: Double standards in clinical trials are ethically unjustifiable; the Declaration of Helsinki requires equivalent ethical standards globally

### Type 2: GMO Position Analysis
> A farmer wants to plant Bt corn that resists stem borers. Analyze the ethical considerations.

**Solution:**
1. **Beneficence**: Higher yields, reduced crop losses, less pesticide use benefits farmers and food security
2. **Non-maleficence**: Potential gene flow to wild relatives, effects on non-target organisms (monarch butterflies), unknown long-term impacts
3. **Justice**: Seed patent costs may disadvantage small farmers; access to technology should be equitable
4. **Autonomy**: Farmers should have choice; neighboring organic farmers' rights must be considered
5. **Buddhist perspective**: Balance between alleviating suffering (hunger) and respecting all living beings (Right Livelihood)
